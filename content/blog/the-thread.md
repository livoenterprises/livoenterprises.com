For most of my working life I've held two beliefs that I thought were separate.

The first: people should own their data. Not license it, not lend it to a platform in exchange for convenience --- own it, the way you own a photograph in a box. You decide who sees it. You decide whether it survives.

The second: devices should work for people, not the other way around. We've spent decades training humans to accommodate machines --- learn the menu, learn the gesture, learn the file format, learn the language. Every one of those is the machine winning. The right direction is the opposite: you say what you want, in the words you already think in, and the machine figures out the rest.

I recently built an app and sat down to write about it, and realized these were never two beliefs. They're one, and it's about autonomy. Owning your data is autonomy over the past --- what you've made and what you keep. Devices that work for you is autonomy over the present --- how you spend your attention and what you're forced to learn. Both are the same refusal: I will not rearrange my life around a system that should be serving me.

The app is called LivoCapsule. It seals family memories --- photos, video, your own voice telling the story --- into plain files that open anywhere, forever, with no account and no server. That's the first belief, made into a thing you can hold.

But the part I didn't expect is that I built it without learning the programming language. I described what I wanted to an AI, in English, and held it to the same engineering discipline I'd used for years --- requirements, design, verification, change control. That's the second belief, applied to the building rather than the using. For the first time the tool met me in my own language, on my own process.

And then a third instance of the thread, which I only noticed writing this: the project itself. Every decision, every requirement, every session's state lives in plain-text documents on my own disk --- not in any AI's memory. I can switch models tomorrow and lose nothing. The project is mine the same way the capsules are mine.

I don't think I'm unusual in holding these beliefs. I think a lot of people hold them and have simply accepted that the technology won't allow it. What I want to say is: the tools have caught up. Not everywhere, not completely, but enough that the thing you've been wanting --- to be served rather than to serve --- is finally available to someone willing to insist on it.

Insist on it.
